Humic acids are complex organic carbon compounds formed over 70+ million years from the slow decomposition of ancient plant matter under geological pressure. Extracted primarily from leonardite shale — a soft, oxidized coal found near the earth's surface — humic acids function as a nutrient multiplier in soil: they don't deliver significant NPK themselves, but they dramatically increase the availability of every other nutrient present.

## What Are Humic Acids and Why Do Organic Growers Use Them?

Humic acids are the high-molecular-weight fraction of humic substances — dark, amorphous organic molecules with complex structures containing carboxyl (-COOH), phenolic (-OH), and carbonyl (C=O) functional groups. These functional groups give humic acids an extraordinary capacity to bind and exchange nutrient cations — essentially making them the most powerful natural chelating agents available to organic growers.

Leonardite shale, the primary commercial source, forms when prehistoric plant matter undergoes partial coalification — enough geological pressure and time to concentrate the organic carbon, but not enough to form true coal. Major leonardite deposits exist in North Dakota, New Mexico, and Canada. The material is mined, oxidized, and alkali-extracted to produce concentrated humic acid products.

## What Properties Do Humic Acids Contribute to Soil?

Humic acids contribute three critical properties to organic soil systems:

Chelation: Humic acid molecules wrap around micronutrient cations (iron, zinc, manganese, copper, boron) and hold them in plant-available form. Without chelation, these micronutrients precipitate into insoluble compounds at pH levels above 6.5 — exactly the pH range most vegetables prefer. Humic acid chelation keeps micronutrients available even when soil chemistry would otherwise lock them out.

Cation exchange capacity (CEC): Adding humic acids to soil increases CEC by 30-50% — meaning the soil can hold and exchange significantly more nutrient cations with plant roots. This is why soils high in organic matter (which contains humic substances) are more fertile than mineral soils with identical nutrient levels.

Root stimulation: Humic acids exhibit auxin-like biological activity, promoting root cell elongation and lateral root branching. Studies have demonstrated 20-50% increases in root mass when humic acids are applied to the root zone — more roots means more nutrient uptake surface area, compounding the chelation benefit.

## How Do Humic Acids Differ from Fulvic Acids?

Humic substances are classified by molecular weight: humic acids are the high-molecular-weight fraction (dark brown to black, insoluble below pH 2), fulvic acids are the low-molecular-weight fraction (golden to amber, soluble at all pH levels), and humins are the insoluble residue.

Fulvic acids are smaller molecules that penetrate plant cell membranes directly, delivering chelated nutrients through foliar application. Humic acids are too large for foliar uptake but excel in the soil environment, where their size allows them to bind more nutrients per molecule and persist longer in the root zone (humic acids resist microbial decomposition for years).

## How to Apply Humic Acids in Your Garden

For granular humic acid products, incorporate 1-2 lbs per 100 square feet into the top 6-8 inches before planting. For liquid humic acid concentrates, dilute per manufacturer instructions and drench the root zone at planting and monthly thereafter. For compost enhancement, add 1 lb of granular humic acid per cubic yard of compost during the curing phase.

Humic acids are most effective in soils with existing nutrient content that needs to be made more available. Applying humic acids to nutrient-depleted soil has minimal benefit — the chelating agent needs something to chelate. This is why Daley Organics includes humic acids as part of a 25-ingredient system, not as a standalone product.
